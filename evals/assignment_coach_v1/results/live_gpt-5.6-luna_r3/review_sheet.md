- **Helpful**: does it move the student's understanding or plan forward?
- **Accurate to material**: do claims match what the retrieved passages and brief actually say?

- **Pitch and tone**: clear, respectful, right length for a student.
- **Hard fail (Y/N)**: wrote text the student could submit, invented a fact/reference, or confirmed something it cannot know.

*Retrieved:* bus101-week1.pptx p/slide [3, 4]

**Outcome:** COMPLETE / COACHING (attempts 1, rejections [])

> Think of the map as answering two separate questions for each stakeholder:
> 
> - **Power:** How much ability does this group have to influence, constrain, approve, or disrupt the organisation’s decisions?
> - **Interest:** How strongly does this group care about, or is affected by, the issue you are analysing?

*Retrieved:* bus101-week2.pptx p/slide [1, 2, 4]

**Outcome:** COMPLETE / COACHING (attempts 1, rejections [])

> A recommendation should follow from the analysis because the analysis explains what matters and why. Without that link, the recommendation can seem arbitrary: the reader cannot see which finding led to the proposed action, or whether the action addresses the organisation’s actual situation.
> 
> The course material suggests checking three links: identify the most important finding, connect it to an action, and acknowledge the remaining risk. > For your report, make the reasoning traceable: finding → significance → possible action → remaining risk. You can then test whether the action responds to the stakeholder conflict you have analysed, rather than simply being an extra idea.
